HOT TAKE

"Emerging" Is Not a Career Stage. It's a Pricing Strategy.

You want to know the dirtiest trick in the art world? It's a single word: emerging.

It sounds supportive. Encouraging, even. Like a butterfly coming out of a cocoon. Like the industry is rooting for you. It's not. "Emerging" is a price tag dressed up as a compliment.

Here's what "emerging" actually means: we like your work enough to sell it, but not enough to pay you fairly for it. It means your paintings go for $3K while the gallery takes $1,500 and tells you to be grateful for the exposure. It means collectors get to feel like visionaries buying "early" while you're subsidizing their investment thesis with your rent money.

The really cynical part? There's no graduation date. No one sends you a letter that says "Congratulations, you've emerged." You can have ten solo shows, three residencies, a museum acquisition, and a feature in Artforum and still get introduced at a dinner as "emerging." MFA at 25. Emerging at 40. Still emerging at 52, apparently, just with better representation and worse knees.

Meanwhile "mid-career" is a ghost town nobody wants to claim, and "established" is reserved for artists who are either dead or selling for six figures. The whole ladder is a fiction. There are three rungs and two of them are broken.

The label exists because it solves a problem, just not yours. It gives galleries a way to justify lower price points. It gives collectors a narrative about discovery. It gives grant committees a box to check. It gives art fairs a category for the smaller booths near the bathroom. Everyone benefits from calling you "emerging" except you.

And the artists who do break out? Half of them didn't "emerge," they got chosen. The right curator saw the right show at the right moment and the market decided you were real. That's not a meritocracy. That's a lottery with better catering.

The fix isn't complicated. Pay artists based on the work, not the label. Price paintings based on what they cost to make and what the market will bear, not based on where someone falls on an imaginary career timeline that resets every time they switch galleries.

Some galleries already get this. They invest in artists for years, build their markets properly, and never use "emerging" like a leash. Those are the ones actually developing careers instead of flipping talent. You know who you are. The rest of you, stop hiding behind a word that was never meant to help anybody but yourselves.

FEATURED ARTIST
Untitled, 2022  •  mixed media on linen

Masie Love

Mixed Media • Washington, D.C. / New York

Masie Love sews her paintings back together like the stories were always supposed to come apart first. Oil and thread on canvas and linen, the stitching not decorative but structural, holding the work the way oral history holds a family. Each pattern is a sentence in a language she's building from scratch, pulled from the time she spent in Goodsprings, Tennessee, from the shapes that surface when you sit with your ancestry long enough to stop romanticizing it and start listening.

The canvases feel inherited, even when they're new. She deconstructs them and reconstructs them, journal pages folded into the process, thread tracing paths across the surface like someone mapping a route home from memory. The "Goodsprings" series is the spine of the work so far, large-scale pieces that vibrate between abstraction and something deeply personal. You can feel Tennessee in them without ever seeing a landscape. The red clay is in the color. The front porches are in the pattern. The people who lived there are in the thread.

Love was born in D.C. in 2000 and is finishing her MFA in Drawing and Painting at Hunter College this spring. She's shown with the Arts Council for Long Beach and is building a practice that already has the conceptual weight most artists spend a decade chasing. At 25, she's making work that treats craft as inheritance and pattern as proof that the people who came before her had something to say. She's just figured out how to let the canvas say it for them.

"I see my process as a form of language; the patterns in my art are the words spoken to me by my ancestors symbolizing fragments given to me through the stories of our past." — Masie Love
