HOT TAKE

Collectors Don't Buy Art. They Buy Stories About Themselves.

Picture this. Opening night. Chelsea. A man walks in wearing a jacket that costs more than the artist's rent. He does a lap. Doesn't read a single placard. Stops in front of the biggest piece - not because it moved him, but because it's the biggest piece. Pulls out his phone. Not to photograph the painting. To photograph himself next to it. Turns to the gallerist and says, "What's the damage?" Buys it in under a minute. Sixty grand. His only question: will it fit above the credenza in the Aspen house. He's gone before the artist even knows the red dot went up. That's not collecting. That's interior decorating with a tax strategy.

Here's how it actually works. Most serious collectors aren't buying art. They're buying a story they get to tell at dinner. "We picked this up at Basel." "She's about to have a show at the Hammer." "We got in early." The painting is a prop in someone else's narrative. And the artists know it. They smile at the openings and shake the right hands and go home and stare at the ceiling wondering if any of it was about the work.

The really ugly part is the flip. A collector buys a piece from an emerging artist for $8K, the artist's market heats up, and that same collector dumps it at auction for $40K two years later. The artist sees none of that money. Zero. They might not even know it happened until someone texts them a screenshot of the lot. The collector made five times their investment. The artist is still paying the same studio rent. And somehow we call this "supporting the arts."

There are real collectors out there. People who live with the work, who build relationships with artists that last decades, who wouldn't dream of flipping a piece like a stock. They exist. But the market isn't built for them. It's built for the credenza guy. And until artists start understanding that distinction, they'll keep getting played by people who see them as an asset class with better dinner party stories.

ARTIST IMAGE

Mark Fleuridor

Painter • Quilter • Miami

Born in Birmingham, Alabama, Mark Fleuridor has been transforming found objects into transcendent sculpture since the late 1970s. His work doesn't ask for permission - it pulls you into a world where industrial waste becomes spiritual artifact, where sandstone carvings carry the weight of generational memory. Now in his seventies, he's also releasing records that sound like transmissions from some other dimension entirely. The art establishment took decades to pay attention. The work didn't care.

Why This Exists

We got tired. That's the honest answer. Tired of watching good artists disappear because they didn't know the right people or couldn't stomach the schmoozing. Tired of an industry that runs on access and pretends it runs on merit. So we built Bernard Studia. Artist representation, digital marketing, web design, paid media, art production - the whole machine, pointed at the work instead of away from it.

This newsletter is the same idea in a different shape. No sponsors. No algorithms. No one telling us what we can and can't say about the industry that pays our rent. Just one conversation a week. Show up or don't. We'll be here either way.

You know somebody we should know about? Got a take that'll make us angry?
Good. Write us. This thing's a conversation, not a monologue.
